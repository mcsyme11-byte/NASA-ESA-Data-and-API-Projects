How to read this

Each ultraviolet picture shows one colour of light, and each colour comes from gas at one temperature, so each shows a different layer. The bright knots are active regions: tangled magnetic fields above sunspots, where flares start. The magnetic picture shows them as black and white patches, the two directions of the field.

How to read this

A disc blocks the bright Sun (the white circle drawn on it shows the Sun's size), so its faint outer atmosphere shows. A coronal mass ejection (CME) is a bright cloud moving outward from one picture to the next. One that spreads all around the disc, a "halo", is coming toward Earth or going away from it. SOHO sends pictures when a ground station is in contact, so the newest can be a few hours old.

Reading NASA's IMAP spacecraft…

How to read this

Below 400 km/s is slow wind. A fast stream from a coronal hole (a dark patch in the 193 Å picture) runs at 500 to 800 km/s; a jump of a hundred km/s or more within minutes is usually a CME's shock arriving.

Magnetic field north–south (Bz)

Reading NASA's IMAP spacecraft…

How to read this

Earth's own field points north. When the solar wind's field points south (below the line), the two join and energy pours in. Hours below about −10 nT bring storms and aurora; while it points north, Earth stays shielded.

Solar wind: NASA's IMAP spacecraft, I-ALiRT real-time data (IMAP Principal Investigator Prof. David J. McComas, Princeton University), through the NASA Community Coordinated Modeling Center's iSWA service.

How to read this

Kp measures how much Earth's magnetic field shook in each 3-hour block, from 13 observatories around the world. Each step up is roughly twice the disturbance, and each step has thirds: 2−, 2 and 2+ (in numbers 1.67, 2 and 2.33). NOAA names storms by Kp: G1 minor at 5, G2 moderate at 6, G3 strong at 7, G4 severe at 8, G5 extreme at 9.

Kp measured: GFZ Helmholtz Centre for Geosciences, CC BY 4.0. Kp forecast: NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center. Both through the NASA Community Coordinated Modeling Center's iSWA service. Storms: NASA DONKI.

Why it matters

Who feels space weather

Satellites

A storm heats and puffs up the upper air, which drags on low satellites. In February 2022 a minor storm brought down 38 of 49 newly launched Starlink satellites. Watch: Kp.

Power grids

A shaking magnetic field pushes currents through long power lines into transformers. On March 13, 1989, Quebec's grid failed in about 90 seconds and six million people were without power for nine hours. Watch: Kp 7 and up, with Bz far south.

GPS

Storms ripple the ionosphere, the charged layer GPS signals pass through, so positions wander. During the May 2024 storm, GPS-guided tractors planting crops in the American Midwest were off by about 3 m (10 ft) instead of a centimetre or two. Watch: Kp.

Radio

A strong flare's X-rays fade out shortwave radio on Earth's daylit side within minutes, which matters to ships and to aircraft over oceans and the poles. NOAA calls an M1 flare a minor radio blackout (R1) and an X1 a strong one (R3). Watch: flare class.

Astronauts

Solar particles can reach dangerous doses outside Earth's magnetic field. The great storms of August 1972 fell between the Apollo 16 and 17 Moon missions, a near miss. Watch: solar energetic particle events.

How to read the numbers

Five rules

1 · Flare class
The letter is a power of ten, the number a multiplier, in watts per square metre of X-rays at Earth: A 10⁻⁸, B 10⁻⁷, C 10⁻⁶, M 10⁻⁵, X 10⁻⁴. Example: an M1.7 is 1.7 × 10⁻⁵ W/m², 17 times a C1.
2 · CME travel time
Hours ≈ 150,000,000 km ÷ speed (km/s) ÷ 3,600. Example: 1,000 km/s gives 42 hours, about 1.7 days. Clouds speed up or slow down toward the solar wind's speed on the way, so most arrive in 1 to 4 days.
3 · Kp
0 to 3 quiet, 4 active, 5 and up a storm (G1 at 5 to G5 at 9). Each step is about twice the disturbance.
4 · Bz
Negative (south) lets the solar wind's energy in; −10 nT or lower for hours makes a storm likely. Positive (north) keeps Earth shielded.
5 · Aurora line
The aurora's edge reaches about 66° − 2° × Kp geomagnetic latitude, and it can be seen low toward the pole from about 4° further from it. Example: at Kp 5 the edge is at 56°, and it shows on the horizon down to about 52°.
