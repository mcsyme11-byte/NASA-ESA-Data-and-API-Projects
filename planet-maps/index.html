    How to read this

    Flat, like a world map. The globe is unrolled so that lines of latitude and longitude form a square grid (an equirectangular map). Places near the poles are stretched sideways, so polar craters look wider than they are.

    Photo or height. "Photo" is thousands of orbiter pictures joined into one. "Height" is coloured by altitude, from blue (low) through green and yellow to red and white (high), and shaded as if lit from one side, so hills and craters stand out.

    Close-ups. Around Jezero and Gale on Mars, and at five of the Apollo sites, much sharper pictures appear as you zoom in. Elsewhere, zooming further only makes the pixels bigger.

    Globe and poles. The globe wraps the same pictures round a ball, the way the world really is. North and South look straight down on a pole (a polar stereographic map), out to 65° of latitude, so the craters and ice caps there keep their true shape.

    Finding a place. A place on the map, or coordinates such as 18.4, 77.5 (latitude, longitude; south and west negative).

    Longitude is counted east from 0° to 180° and west to −180°, the convention NASA uses for its maps of both worlds.

    The method

    How these maps were made

    1. Photograph strip by strip. An orbiter circles from pole to pole while the world turns beneath it, so each orbit adds a new strip. NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter photographed the whole Moon for this map in more than 15,000 pictures between 2009 and 2011.
    2. Measure the height. A laser altimeter fires pulses at the ground and times their return. MOLA, on Mars Global Surveyor, made more than 600 million measurements of Mars between 1999 and 2001; LOLA does the same for the Moon from the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter.
    3. Join and flatten. The pictures are fitted to the height map, corrected for the angle they were taken from, and joined into one mosaic on a latitude–longitude grid.
    4. Cut into tiles. NASA's Solar System Treks service cuts each mosaic into squares of 256 pixels at every zoom level. The map loads only the squares on the screen; each step in zoom has four times as many.

    Rules: how sharp is a map?

    Rule 1
    One degree of longitude at the equator is 2π × radius ÷ 360. Moon: 2π × 1,737.4 km ÷ 360 = 30.3 km. Mars: 2π × 3,396.2 km ÷ 360 = 59.3 km.
    Rule 2
    When a map is given in pixels per degree: metres per pixel = one degree in metres ÷ pixels per degree. The Moon photo, 303 pixels per degree: 30,323 m ÷ 303 = 100 m a pixel.
    Rule 3
    When it is given in metres per pixel: the smallest thing you can make out is about 3 pixels across. The Jezero close-up, 25 cm a pixel: about 75 cm, a large rock. Perseverance is 3 m long.
    Rule 4
    Away from the equator, a degree of longitude shrinks: multiply by the cosine of the latitude. At Phoenix's landing site, 68° N on Mars: 59.3 km × cos 68° = 22.2 km.
