How to read this

Flat, like a world map. The globe is unrolled so that lines of latitude and longitude form a square grid. Places near the poles are stretched sideways, so polar craters look wider than they are.

Photo or height. "Photo" is thousands of orbiter pictures joined into one. "Height" is coloured by altitude (blue low, red and white high) and shaded as if lit from one side, so hills and craters stand out.

Close-ups. Around Jezero, Gale and the Apollo sites, much sharper pictures appear as you zoom in. Outside them, zooming further only makes the pixels bigger.

The method

How these maps were made

  1. Photograph strip by strip. An orbiter circles from pole to pole while the world turns beneath it, so each orbit adds a new strip. NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter photographs the whole Moon in a month.
  2. Measure the height. A laser altimeter fires pulses at the ground and times their return: MOLA on Mars Global Surveyor made more than 600 million measurements of Mars, LOLA on the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter does the same for the Moon.
  3. Join and flatten. The pictures are fitted to the height map, corrected for the angle they were taken from, and joined into one mosaic on a latitude–longitude grid.
  4. Cut into tiles. NASA's Trek service cuts each mosaic into squares of 256 pixels at every zoom level, so the map loads only what is on the screen.

Rules: how sharp is a map?

Rule 1
One degree of longitude at the equator is 2π × radius ÷ 360. Moon: 2π × 1,737.4 km ÷ 360 = 30.3 km. Mars: 2π × 3,396.2 km ÷ 360 = 59.3 km.
Rule 2
When a map is given in pixels per degree: metres per pixel = one degree in metres ÷ pixels per degree. The Moon photo, 303 pixels per degree: 30,323 m ÷ 303 = 100 m a pixel.
Rule 3
When it is given in metres per pixel: the smallest thing you can make out is about 3 pixels across. The Jezero close-up, 25 cm a pixel: about 75 cm, a large rock. Perseverance is 3 m long.
