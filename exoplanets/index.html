Six ways to find a planet you can't see

A planet like Earth is about ten billion times fainter than its star in visible light. Almost every method watches the star instead.

Method 1 · … planets

Transit

The planet crosses in front of its star and blocks a little of its light. The dip gives the planet's size; the time between dips gives its year.

Kepler · TESSCHEOPS · PLATOOpen
Method 2 · … planets

Radial velocity

The planet's pull makes the star wobble towards and away from us. The wobble shifts the star's light, blue then red, and gives the planet's minimum mass.

Ground telescopesOpen
Method 3 · … planets

Microlensing

A star passing in front of a more distant star bends and brightens its light for a few weeks; a planet adds a short extra blip.

Roman, launched 2026Open
Method 4 · … planets

Direct imaging

Block the star's glare and photograph the planet itself: young, hot giants far from their stars, like the four planets of HR 8799.

Webb · HubbleOpen
Method 5 · … planets

Astrometry

Measure the star's tiny loop across the sky. ESA's Gaia does this for nearly two billion stars; its fourth data release, due on Dec 2, 2026, includes a catalogue of planet candidates.

GaiaOpen
Method 6 · … planets

Timing

Something that should tick like a clock runs early, then late: a pulsar's radio pulses, or another planet's transits. The first planets ever confirmed, in 1992, were found this way.

Method 1

The transit method, step by step

From the dip to the planet

The planet blocks the part of the star's disc it covers, so the fraction of light lost, the depth δ, is the ratio of their areas: δ = (Rp ÷ R★)². Turned round, the planet's radius is Rp = R★ × √δ.

Earth crossing the Sun would dim it by only 0.0084%, 84 parts in a million; Jupiter by about 1%.

How to read this

Start in the first column with what was measured; the formula gives what is in the second. Use the units under each formula exactly as written. The examples use the values in NASA's Exoplanet Archive on Nov 17, 2026, so they match the archive to the rounding shown.

For the habitable zone, use NASA's rule first: it is simple and works for stars like the Sun. Then use the second rule for cooler and hotter stars, whose light warms a planet differently. The explorer gives every planet's answer under both.
