How planets are found

The transit method is on the overview. Here are the other five ways, each with what it measures, the rule that turns the measurement into a planet, and what it has found.

Method 2 · … planets

Radial velocity: the star's wobble

The first planet around a star like the Sun, 51 Pegasi b, was found this way in 1995.

From the wobble to the planet

A star and its planet both go round their shared centre of mass. The heavier the planet and the closer its orbit, the faster the star's wobble. The wobble's size, K, and the orbit's period P give the planet's mass, with one catch: only the part of the motion towards and away from us is seen, so the answer is a minimum mass, written Mp sin i, where i is the orbit's tilt.

The rule (circular orbit, planet much lighter than its star):
Mp sin i = K ÷ 28.4 × M★2/3 × P1/3, with Mp in Jupiter masses, K in m/s, M★ in Suns and P in years. 28.4 m/s is the wobble a Jupiter-mass planet causes in a one-year orbit around the Sun.

Jupiter makes the Sun wobble by 12.7 m/s, about walking pace; Earth by only 0.09 m/s. The best spectrographs today measure to a few tenths of a m/s, so Earth twins are still out of reach.

Method 3 · … planets

Microlensing: a star as a magnifying glass

How it works

Gravity bends light. When a star passes almost exactly in front of a more distant star, it acts as a lens and brightens the distant star for a few weeks. If the lens star has a planet, the planet's own small lens adds a short extra brightening, lasting hours to a few days.

The rules. The length of the whole event grows with the square root of the lens star's mass; the length of the planet's blip grows with the square root of the planet's mass. So the blip's length compared with the event's gives the planet-to-star mass ratio: q = (tplanet ÷ tevent)², roughly. Each event happens once and is never seen again.

The planet of OGLE-2005-BLG-390 is about 5.5 times Earth's mass, at about 2.6 au from a small, faint star some 21,500 light years away (Beaulieu et al. 2006), one of the first cold, low-mass planets found. NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, launched in August 2026, will watch hundreds of millions of stars towards the centre of the galaxy for events like this one.

Method 4 · … planets

Direct imaging: a picture of the planet

See pictures

A planet is a faint speck right beside a dazzling star. A coronagraph inside the telescope blocks the star's light, and the planet's own glow is photographed in infrared. It works best for young, hot giant planets far from their stars: they still glow from their formation, and they sit far enough out to be seen apart from the star.

The rule: how far apart they look. θ = a ÷ d, with θ in arcseconds, the orbit a in au and the distance d in parsecs.

The rule: how faint. In visible light, Earth is about 10−10 as bright as the Sun (a ten-billionth), Jupiter about 10−9. Young giants glowing in infrared are only about 10−4 to 10−6 as bright as their stars, which is why they are found first. NASA's planned Habitable Worlds Observatory is being designed to reach 10−10, to photograph Earth-like planets.

Pictures of HR 8799's planets and others taken by Webb, Hubble and Keck are in NASA's picture library: open the Image Explorer's picture library and search for "HR 8799" or "exoplanet".

Method 5 · … planets

Astrometry: the star's loop across the sky

ESA Gaia

The same pull that makes a star wobble towards and away from us also moves it sideways, in a tiny loop on the sky. Measuring that loop needs extraordinarily precise positions: ESA's Gaia spacecraft has measured the positions of nearly two billion stars, again and again, since 2014.

The rule. α = (Mp ÷ M★) × a ÷ d, with α the size of the star's loop in arcseconds, a in au and d in parsecs. Unlike radial velocity, astrometry gives the planet's true mass, not a minimum, because it sees the whole orbit's shape.

Example. Jupiter has 0.000954 of the Sun's mass and orbits at 5.2 au. Seen from 10 parsecs (32.6 light years): α = 0.000954 × 5.2 ÷ 10 = 0.0005″, half a thousandth of an arcsecond. Gaia measures the brightest stars' positions finely enough to see loops of that size.

Gaia's fourth data release, due on Dec 2, 2026, covers its first 5.5 years of measurements and includes a catalogue of exoplanet candidates. They will be added here once released, kept apart from the confirmed planets until they are confirmed.

Method 6 · … planets

Timing: a clock that runs early, then late

Some things in space keep extraordinarily regular time. A pulsar, the spinning core left by an exploded star, sends a radio pulse with every turn. When a planet pulls the pulsar back and forth, the pulses arrive a little early, then a little late, because their light has a little less, then a little more, distance to travel. The first planets ever confirmed, in 1992, were found this way around the pulsar PSR B1257+12.

The rule. A shift in arrival time Δt means the star moved c × Δt along our line of sight, where c is the speed of light, 299,792 km/s. A shift of 1 millisecond is a movement of about 300 km.

The same idea works for a planet's transits (transit timing): a second, unseen planet tugs the first, so its transits come early or late by minutes. Eclipsing double stars (eclipse timing) and stars that pulse regularly (pulsation timing) are used in the same way.
