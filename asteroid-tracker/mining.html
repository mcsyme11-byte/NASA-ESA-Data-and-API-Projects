The asteroids easiest to reach

How to read this: the rules

Rule 1, round trip is the total change in speed (delta-v) a spacecraft needs: leaving a 400 km orbit around Earth, matching the asteroid's speed, leaving it again, and slowing down for Earth's atmosphere. For comparison, a round trip to orbit around the Moon needs about 5 km/s, and a landing on the Moon and back about 9 km/s.

Rule 2, trip length is for the cheapest trip, including at least 8 days at the asteroid. Faster trips exist, but cost more delta-v.

Rule 3, made of is the measured spectral type (its colour in sunlight). Most small asteroids have never had theirs measured, so "Unknown" is the usual answer, not a fault.

Rule 4, size is a measurement where one exists; otherwise a range worked out from brightness, because a dark asteroid is bigger than a bright one of the same brightness.

Rule 5, cheapest launch is the launch date of the cheapest trip still ahead, where JPL's launch windows for the asteroid are on this site; the others open on JPL's own page.

Rule 6, the list changes. New discoveries join it, and better orbits move asteroids up, down or off it. It is a study of what is possible, not a list of planned missions.

Could we mine one?

How the verdict is worked out

the same rules on every asteroid's page
Reach
NHATS's cheapest round trip. Up to 5 km/s: about as easy as a trip to orbit around the Moon. 5 to 9 km/s: between that and a Moon landing. 9 to 12 km/s: harder than a Moon landing. Not on the list: no round trip within NHATS's limits.
Material
The measured type, by group. Carbon-rich (B, C, Cb, Cg, Ch, F, G): water bound in clay, and carbon. Stony (S, Sq, Q, K, L, A): rock with grains of nickel-iron. X group (X, Xc, Xe, Xk), split by albedo: below 0.1 dark rock, 0.1 to 0.3 possibly metal-rich, above 0.3 enstatite. V: basalt.
Size and spin
Under 10 m: a boulder, a target for a quick look, not for mining. Spinning once in less than 2.2 hours: faster than loose rubble can hold together, so probably one solid rock, harder to land on.
Mining today
Always "not realistic today": no mission has yet extracted resources from an asteroid, and the most brought back so far is 121.6 g. The verdict says how good a target an asteroid is for the next step, a sample mission.
The maths: why every km/s counts

The rocket equation. mass at start ÷ mass at end = eΔv ÷ ve, where Δv is the change in speed and ve the speed of the engine's exhaust. Use it to find how much of a spacecraft must be propellant.

Example. Δv = 5.0 km/s with a hydrogen-oxygen engine (ve ≈ 4.4 km/s): e5.0 ÷ 4.4 = 3.1, so 1 − 1 ÷ 3.1 = 68% of the spacecraft leaving Earth orbit is propellant. At 9 km/s it is 87%: each extra km/s costs more than the one before.

Mass from size. mass = (π ÷ 6) × diameter³ × density. Example, Bennu: (π ÷ 6) × (484 m)³ × 1,194 kg/m³ = 7.1 × 1010 kg, about 71 million tonnes.
