Mega-constellations (Starlink, OneWeb, Kuiper, Qianfan, Guowang) are working satellites too; switch them off to see the rest.

    Tap a dot on the globe, or find an object by name, to see what it is, how high it flies and its orbit.

    How to read this

    Each dot is one object, placed where it is at the time shown at the bottom left, worked out in your browser from its latest published orbital elements with the SGP4 model, the same model the elements are made for. Dots are not to scale: the Earth is 12,742 km across and most satellites are a few metres.

    What is on the globe: every satellite CelesTrak lists as working (more than half of them in the mega-constellations), and the three largest debris clouds it publishes as groups: the Chinese weather satellite Fengyun-1C, destroyed in a missile test in 2007, and the Russian Cosmos 2251 and the American Iridium 33, which collided in 2009. Dead satellites, rocket bodies and other debris are counted in the chart below, but CelesTrak does not publish their orbits as one list, so most of them are not drawn.

    Heights: the thin shell close to the ground is low Earth orbit (up to 2,000 km), where almost everything is. Zoom out to see the navigation satellites around 20,000 km and the ring of geostationary satellites at 35,786 km, which go round once a day and so stay over one place.

    Speed: real time shows the satellites moving at their true pace (a low satellite circles the Earth in about 90 minutes); ×60 makes a minute pass each second. The Earth turns and the night side moves with the time shown.

    Keyboard: select the globe (click it or press Tab), then the arrow keys turn it, + and − zoom, Home goes back to the start and Escape clears the selection. Drag to turn, scroll or pinch to zoom.

    Out under the real sky? Sky Finder shows when the space stations pass over your place tonight. Missions (telescopes, crewed stations, probes to the planets) are on the spacecraft map.

    How to read this

    Each bar is one band of height, 50 km thick, starting at the height under it. An object is put in the band of its mean height, half-way between its lowest and highest point. Below about 600 km the thin outer air pulls things down within a few years; 500 to 600 km holds most of today's working satellites; 750 to 1,000 km holds most of the old debris, where it stays for centuries (NASA Orbital Debris Program Office).
