Close calls and collisions

Objects in orbit pass close to each other thousands of times a week. Almost always they miss. When two do hit, at around 10 km/s, they become a cloud of hundreds or thousands of pieces, and each piece can hit something else.

How to read this

Each row is a prediction worked out by CelesTrak's SOCRATES Plus from the two objects' published orbits, for every pass within 5 km over the next seven days. Miss distance is the predicted gap at the closest moment. Chance of hitting is the largest chance the prediction allows: the published orbits are only known to within a few hundred metres to a few kilometres, so it is an upper limit, not a forecast.

A working satellite that can move usually does when a pass looks risky. A dead satellite, a rocket body or a piece of debris cannot, so two of them simply take their chances. Watch opens the pass on the globe, five minutes before the closest moment.

The rules the calculator follows

Rule 1, how hard the hit is. The energy of the smaller object for each kilogram of the bigger one: ε = msmall × v² ÷ (2 × mbig), with v in metres per second, in joules per kilogram (÷ 1,000 for joules per gram).

Rule 2, which mass breaks up. If ε is more than 40 joules per gram, the collision is catastrophic: both objects break up, and the mass that turns into pieces is M = mbig + msmall (in kg). If not, the bigger object is only cratered, and M = msmall × v², with v in kilometres per second.

Rule 3, how many pieces. The number of pieces bigger than a size L (in metres) is N = 0.1 × M0.75 × L−1.71: L = 0.1 for pieces over 10 cm, L = 0.01 for pieces over 1 cm.

Example: the 2009 crash. Iridium 33 (560 kg) and Cosmos 2251 (about 900 kg) met at 11.7 km/s. Rule 1: ε = 560 × 11,700² ÷ (2 × 900) ≈ 42.6 million J/kg = 42,600 J/g. Rule 2: far above 40, so catastrophic, and M = 900 + 560 = 1,460 kg. Rule 3: N = 0.1 × 1,4600.75 × 0.1−1.71 ≈ 1,200 pieces over 10 cm. The tracking network catalogued 2,372, so the model gives the size of the problem, not an exact count.

The cloud is an illustration: each piece leaves with a slightly different speed, so the pieces spread along the orbit within hours and right round the Earth within days; over months the Earth's bulge turns their orbits apart, and the cloud becomes a shell around the whole planet.

The chain reaction

The Kessler syndrome

a teaching model, not a forecast

Try it

In 1978 the NASA scientist Donald Kessler showed that once a height is crowded enough, the pieces from each crash cause new crashes faster than the air can clear them, even if nothing more is launched.

How the model works

One band of height starts with 1,500 whole objects (satellites and rocket bodies) and 1,500 pieces of debris. Each year: the new satellites are added and the removed ones taken away; the air brings down a share of everything, which depends on the height (half in about a year and a half at 400 km, in about 8 years at 550 km, in about a century at 800 km); and collisions happen in proportion to the number of whole objects times the number of all objects, each one turning a whole object into 800 pieces (Rule 3, rounded down).

It is far simpler than the agencies' own models, which follow every object, but shows the same three lessons: low down, the air cleans up by itself; high up, the number can grow even with no launches; and removing a few large dead objects a year slows the growth more than anything else. NASA's own studies found that removing about five large objects a year from the most crowded orbits would keep the population steady, and ESA's 2026 report says the same: stop making debris, and start removing it.
