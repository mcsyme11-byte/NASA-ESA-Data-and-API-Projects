Cleaning up

Nothing in orbit is ever thrown away. It stays up until the thin outer air slows it enough to fall and burn up, which can take a few years or many centuries. Cleaning up means helping things come down sooner, and making no more junk.

How to read this

The air thins out with height but never quite stops. Each lap through it slows an object a little, which lowers its orbit, where the air is thicker, so the fall speeds up near the end. Light objects with a large surface (a sheet of foil) come down faster than dense ones (a solid metal block). The Sun matters too: in the busy years of its 11-year cycle it heats and puffs up the outer air, and objects come down sooner.

The ways

How it can be done

  1. Make no more

    Empty fuel tanks and drain batteries when a mission ends, so a dead satellite or stage cannot explode years later; release no covers, bolts or straps. ESA's "Zero Debris" approach aims to stop making new debris by 2030.

  2. Leave on time

    Bring a satellite down within 5 years of the end of its mission (the rule in the United States since 2022, and ESA's since 2023), or move it out of the busy orbits. Most new satellites keep fuel for this.

  3. Catch and drag down

    A chaser meets a dead object, matches its spin, catches it with robot arms, a net, a harpoon or a docking plate, and fires its engine to pull both into the air to burn up. Large dead objects come first: each one is a future cloud.

  4. Slow it from a distance

    Drag sails and tethers fitted before launch make a dead satellite fall faster. Pushing small pieces lower with lasers from the ground has been studied, but not done.

Why so few, and why so hard

No mission has yet removed a piece of junk it did not launch itself: ADRAS-J2 and ClearSpace-1 aim to be the first. Real junk was never built to be caught. It has no handles, it may be tumbling, and nobody knows exactly what shape it is in after years in space. A chaser has to come within metres of it without making things worse. ClearSpace-1 changed its target in 2024, after its first one, a payload adapter left in orbit by a 2013 launch, was itself struck by a small piece of debris that broke off more pieces.

Why it matters

What we would lose

Weather forecasts, satellite navigation, phone and internet links far from cables, watching the climate, crops and disasters, and the people living on the space stations all depend on orbits staying usable.

ESA's 2026 Space Environment Report counts more than 300 launches in 2025, with over 4,000 new satellites, about ten a day, and finds that the long-term strain on orbit grew about tenfold in one year. Its advice is the same as NASA's: stop making debris, bring satellites down on time, and start removing the largest dead objects.

The biggest first

Removing a few large dead objects a year does more than chasing thousands of small pieces, because each large one is a future cloud.

Too small to see

ESA's model estimates 1.5 million pieces between 1 and 10 cm: too small to track, so a satellite cannot move out of their way.
